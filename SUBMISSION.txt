Abstract
When the activist group Stop AI blockaded OpenAI's headquarters, they presented two distinct demands: 1) a prohibition on advanced AI development, and 2) an investigation into the death of a former employee. The dual focus gave journalists multiple angles to cover. Here, we examine how news media conveyed the movement's proposed AI restrictions, and how later reports interpreted the jury's decision to convict a participating protester. Through analyzing 24 news texts, we found that 19 mentioned an AI intervention, but only 9 of those 19 stated that it should be permanent. Coverage often identified an intervention without explaining how long it would last, even though duration distinguished a permanent prohibition from a conditional pause. We also found that when covering the trial, five out of six articles carried a claim, explicit or implicit and often voiced by a quoted speaker, that the jury rejected the protester's arguments. All five trace back to three origins: the prosecutor's statement and reporting by The Standard and The Guardian. None of the six mentioned the movement's claim that the judge withheld a necessity instruction, which we could not verify against official instructions. We compare the Stop AI case with documented judicial records from the M25 climate protests and the Colston statue removal trials for context, comparisons that show a general verdict does not reveal a jury's underlying reasoning.
